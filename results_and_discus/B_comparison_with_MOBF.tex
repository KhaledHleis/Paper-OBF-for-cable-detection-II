\subsection{Comparison with the Multipolar Framework}

In complement to the experimental and simulation results, this subsection examines the detection performance and associated energy capture properties.

In the MOBF study \cite{chenevas2025multipolar}, it was shown that the receiver whose order matches the source is not necessarily the best-performing one. Because the source coefficients are unknown, the GLRT reduces to a matched-subspace (energy) detector rather than a true matched filter. Among such subspace detectors, projecting onto a lower-order subspace can yield superior detection when that subspace already captures most of the signal energy, since it involves fewer noise-dominated dimensions. This reflects a trade-off between captured signal energy and the number of noise dimensions admitted.

In other words, even when the MOBF does not match the cable signature, cable detection remains possible. The two experiments presented below are designed to investigate this phenomenon.

In both the MOBF framework and the present work, the detectors are structurally very similar, the only difference lies in the choice of templates. Specifically, we define the test statistic
\begin{equation}
    T_f = \sum_{p=1}^{P} \langle x, g_p \rangle^2,
\end{equation}
where $T_f$ denotes the filter output, $P$ is the number of basis function, and $g_p$ denotes one of the OBF.

\subsubsection{experiment 1, energy capture vs. MOBF order}
We took $S(t)$, by de-normalizing equation \ref{eq:anomaly}. For each multipolar order M=1 (Dipole/Anderson),2(Quadrupole), 3 (Octopole), we built the MOBF (see table \ref{tab:basis_comparison}) and computed the fraction of $S$'s energy that lives in the subspace spanned by the MOBF.
\begin{equation}
    \eta (M) = \frac{T_f}{\sum_t S(t)^2}
\end{equation}

To calculate $S(t)$ we took arbitrarily realistic parameters ($v=1.8~m/s, \ d=1~m, \ \theta =90^\circ,\ \psi=45^\circ,\ \delta=60^\circ$), to calculate $\eta$ table \ref{tab:energy_capture}, by construction, $\eta_{cable} = 1$ for our cable OBF.

The results show that $\eta(M) < 1$ for every finite, numerically usable M, and raising M helps only marginally (the basis functions decay ever faster, beyond M=3, rendering them impractical \cite{chenevas2025multipolar}). This is to be expected, because the cable does not act as a multipole of any finite order, its field decreases too slowly compared with that of any multipole, which will be discussed in more detail in \ref{sec:SnP}.


\subsubsection{ROC and SNR}

Building on the previous results, we evaluated the ROC curve, see figure~\ref{fig:ROCvsSNR}, using the analytical expression from~\cite{Kay1998Detection},
\[
P_d = \bar F_{\chi'^2_v(\lambda)}\bigl(\bar F_{\chi^2_v}^{-1}(P_{fa})\bigr),
\]
where $\bar{F}(\cdot) = 1 - F(\cdot)$ is the complementary CDF, $v$ the subspace dimension (number of basis functions), and $\lambda$ the non-centrality parameter $\lambda = T_f/\sigma^2$, validated against a Monte Carlo simulation with $2\times 10^4$ realizations. For each SNR we computed the area under the curve (AUC), see figure~\ref{fig:AUCvsSNR}.

Disregarding the detection offset discussed in section~\ref{sec:simulation}, the results here indicate that the MOBF is capable of detecting the cable with performance compared to that of the matched basis. However, they also show that the matched cable OBF consistently outperforms or matches all considered MOBF orders, with the performance gap becoming particularly pronounced at SNR values below \(-15\)~dB. This behavior is attributable to the fact that the cable OBF captures the full energy of the signal \(s\) while involving the minimum number of noisy channels.

Finally, the behavior observed in Figure~\ref{fig:AUCvsSNR} is reproduced in Figure~\ref{fig:paramJumble} for different combinations of \(\psi\) and \(\delta\), providing further evidence that the cable OBF outperforms the MOBF for cable detection.
